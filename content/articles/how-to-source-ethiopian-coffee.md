Buying coffee from Ethiopia is rewarding, but most problems in an international coffee purchase can be traced back to information nobody asked for at the start. This guide lists what to request, and why, before you order. It is general buying guidance: exact answers always depend on the specific coffee, exporter and shipment.

**In short, before you order you should know:**

- who sells the coffee to you, who ships it and who invoices you;
- exactly which coffee you are buying: origin, process, grade and crop year;
- what quality evidence exists, who produced it and when;
- which samples you will receive, and how they relate to the coffee that ships;
- the quantity, packaging, price basis, shipping terms and payment terms;
- which documents will accompany the shipment, and what your own market requires.

## 1. Who is selling, shipping and invoicing?

Coffee exports from Ethiopia are regulated by the Ethiopian Coffee and Tea Authority (ECTA), and coffee is exported by licensed exporters. Ask for the full legal name of the exporter who will sell and ship the coffee, and confirm who will be the party to your sales contract and who will issue the invoice.

If you work through an agent or sourcing company, ask them to explain their role in writing: whom they represent, how they are paid, and which responsibilities stay with the exporter. A clear answer here prevents confusion later about quality claims, documents and payments.

## 2. Exactly which coffee are you buying?

"Ethiopian coffee" covers a very wide range of flavours and qualities. Ask for a precise description of the lot being offered:

- **Origin:** region and, where known, the zone, district or locality.
- **Source:** washing station, cooperative or producer, if the exporter can identify it.
- **Process:** washed, natural or another method.
- **Grade:** the Ethiopian export grade (for example Grade 1 or Grade 2). Ethiopian grades combine physical and cup assessment; grades 1 and 2 are commonly associated with higher-quality lots, but the grade alone does not describe the flavour.
- **Crop year:** when the coffee was harvested and processed.
- **Lot identifier:** the reference that will appear on samples, the contract and shipping documents.

If some of these details are unavailable, ask why. Some coffee is sold with regional rather than washing-station-level traceability, and that can be perfectly acceptable, as long as you know what you are buying.

## 3. What quality evidence exists?

Quality claims are only as useful as the evidence behind them. For each lot, ask:

- **Cupping results:** tasting notes and any score, together with who evaluated the coffee, when, and which protocol they used. The Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) adopted its Coffee Value Assessment as its cupping standard in 2024, replacing the 2004 cupping form, so scores from different evaluators and forms may not be directly comparable.
- **Physical analysis:** moisture content, water activity, screen size and defect count. The International Coffee Organization's recommended range for green coffee moisture is 8% to 12.5%; coffee outside that range carries a higher risk of quality loss.
- **Photos** of the green coffee, ideally with the lot reference visible.
- **Date of the analysis**, because green coffee changes over time.

## 4. Which samples will you receive?

Samples are how you connect a description to the physical coffee. The terms vary slightly between companies, but the common types are:

| Sample type | What it represents | When it is used |
|---|---|---|
| Type sample | A general quality level or a past lot, not a specific parcel | Early discussions, to understand a supplier's range |
| Offer sample | A specific lot that is available for sale now | Deciding whether to buy |
| Pre-shipment sample | The finished, export-ready coffee from the lot about to ship | Confirming quality before the coffee is loaded |
| Arrival sample | Coffee taken after it lands at destination | Confirming what was delivered |

Ask which of these the exporter provides, the sample size, who pays for samples and courier costs, and whether shipment depends on your approval of a pre-shipment sample. Agree the process in writing.

## 5. Quantity and packaging

Confirm the quantity in unambiguous units. Green coffee from Ethiopia is commonly packed in 60 kg bags, but always check the bag weight, the number of bags and the total net weight.

Ask about packaging: jute bags alone, or with hermetic liners, and whether that affects price or lead time. Packaging choices matter for how the coffee holds up in transit and storage.

## 6. Price, shipping and payment terms

A price is only meaningful with its basis. A quotation should state:

- the **price and currency**, and the unit (for example per kg or per pound);
- the **shipping term** (Incoterm), such as FOB or CIF, and the named port;
- the **shipment window** and how long the offer is valid;
- the **payment terms**, for example payment against documents or a letter of credit;
- what happens if the coffee is delayed or does not match the approved sample.

Under the International Chamber of Commerce's Incoterms 2020 rules, FOB and CIF both transfer risk to the buyer once the goods are on board the vessel at the port of shipment. The difference is who arranges and pays for the main sea freight and insurance. The ICC notes that FCA and CIP are designed for containerised cargo, so discuss with your freight forwarder which term suits your shipment.

## 7. Documents and your market's requirements

Ask the exporter which documents normally accompany a shipment. These typically include a commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, certificate of origin and phytosanitary certificate, and sometimes weight and quality certificates.

Then check your own market. For example, food imported into the United States requires registered facilities and prior notice of shipments to the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). Importers into the European Union should check what due-diligence information they need under the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR). Its application dates differ by company size, so consult the European Commission's current guidance. Collecting geolocation data from a supplier is part of that work, not the whole of it. Your customs broker or regulatory adviser is the right source for requirements specific to your business.

## 8. Certifications and claims

If you need certified coffee (for example organic or Fairtrade), ask for the certificate itself: the certificate holder, its scope and its validity dates. Certification applies to specific operators and products, so a certificate held by one company does not automatically cover every coffee it sells.

## A checklist you can copy

| Topic | What to ask for |
|---|---|
| Seller | Exporter's legal name, contract party, invoicing party |
| Coffee | Region, source, process, grade, crop year, lot reference |
| Quality | Cupping notes and score with evaluator, date and protocol; moisture, water activity, defects |
| Samples | Types offered, size, cost, courier, pre-shipment approval |
| Quantity | Number of bags, bag weight, total net weight, packaging |
| Terms | Price basis, Incoterm and port, shipment window, validity, payment |
| Documents | Documents supplied by the exporter; your market's import requirements |
| Claims | Certificates with holder, scope and validity |

Ethiopian coffee can be bought unroasted ("green") or already roasted. The right choice depends less on the coffee itself than on your business: whether you roast, how fast you sell, where the coffee is going and what your market requires. This guide compares the two formats to help you decide what to ask for. It is general guidance, not a description of any particular supplier's terms.

**In short:**

- **Green coffee** suits businesses that roast, or that sell to roasters. It is more stable in transit and storage than roasted coffee, and it gives you control over roasting and branding. You need roasting capacity and expertise.
- **Roasted coffee** suits businesses that sell coffee ready to brew, such as distributors, retailers and hospitality groups. You don't need to roast, but freshness, packaging, labelling and food-import rules all matter from the day it is roasted.

## Side-by-side comparison

| | Green coffee | Roasted coffee |
|---|---|---|
| Typical buyers | Importers, green traders, roasters | Distributors, wholesalers, retailers, hospitality businesses |
| What you control | Roast profile, blending, grind, packaging, brand | Mostly choice of product and quantity. Product details are set with the supplier |
| Stability | Relatively stable if moisture and storage conditions are right, but quality still fades over time | Flavour declines noticeably after roasting, especially once exposed to oxygen |
| Transit sensitivity | Moderate: needs dry, well-ventilated shipping and storage | High: every week in transit is part of the product's usable life |
| Equipment and skills | Roaster, quality control, storage | Storage, and a sales channel that moves stock quickly |
| Regulation at destination | Import rules for agricultural or food products apply | Usually treated as a packaged food: labelling and registration rules often apply |
| Planning rhythm | Often bought around harvest and shipment periods | Often bought in repeat orders sized to sales |

## When green coffee makes sense

Green coffee is the traditional way coffee is traded internationally. The International Coffee Organization's guidance for exported coffee is a moisture content between 8% and 12.5%. Within that range, and with suitable storage, green coffee can travel long distances and be held for months, although its cup quality will still gradually fade.

Buying green gives you flexibility. You can roast to your customers' tastes, blend, adjust roast levels, and pack under your own brand in your own market, close to your customers. The trade-off is that you need roasting equipment, skills and quality control, and you carry the risk of storing green coffee until you use it.

Questions to ask yourself:

- Do we roast now, or plan to within the next year?
- Can we store green coffee in cool, dry conditions?
- Can we evaluate samples ourselves, or do we need help?
- How much coffee will we use in a year, and how often do we want to reorder?

## When roasted coffee makes sense

Buying roasted coffee lets you sell Ethiopian coffee without roasting it yourself. For some distributors and hospitality groups, that is the whole point.

The central challenge is freshness. Roasted coffee releases carbon dioxide after roasting and becomes stale mainly through oxidation. That is why roasted coffee is usually packed in bags with one-way degassing valves, which let gas out without letting air in, and why some roasters flush packs with nitrogen to reduce oxygen. The packaging, the time between roasting and dispatch, the shipping time and storage all affect how the coffee tastes when it reaches your customers.

Roasted coffee is also generally treated as a packaged food product in the destination market. In the European Union, for example, food information to consumers is governed by Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, and in the United States imported food requires FDA facility registration and prior notice. Labels, languages, business names and dates may all need to meet local rules. Check with your regulatory adviser before ordering.

Questions to ask yourself:

- How quickly will we sell each shipment after it arrives?
- Do we (or our importer) already import packaged food into this market?
- Do we need whole bean, ground, or both? In which pack sizes?
- Do we want an existing product, or do we have branding requirements that would need to be discussed with the supplier?

## Information to request for each format

**For green coffee:** origin, process, grade, crop year, lot reference, cupping results (with evaluator and date), moisture and defect information, sample options, bag type and weight, shipping terms and shipment window. Our guide [How to source Ethiopian coffee](/resources/how-to-source-ethiopian-coffee-information-to-request) covers these in detail.

**For roasted coffee:** origin and composition (single origin or blend), roast level, whole bean or ground, pack sizes and materials (including valves and any nitrogen flushing), roast date and batch coding, the supplier's stated shelf life and storage guidance, production lead time, labelling, and the documents the supplier can provide for your market.

## Can you combine both?

Some businesses do. For example, a distributor may buy roasted coffee for one market while a partner roaster buys green coffee for another, or a business may start with roasted coffee and move to green once it has roasting capacity. If you are unsure, it helps to describe your customers, volumes and destination first and decide the format from there.

Samples are where a coffee purchase becomes concrete. Evaluating them consistently, and explaining clearly what you liked and what you didn't, is the fastest way to reach a coffee that fits your business. This guide covers how to brief a supplier, what to check when samples arrive, and how to give useful feedback. It is general guidance for business buyers; each supplier's sample terms need to be confirmed individually.

## Step 1: Write down your requirement before asking for samples

A short written brief saves rounds of back-and-forth. It doesn't need to be perfect, and "not sure yet" is a useful answer too.

| Item | Example of a useful answer |
|---|---|
| Product | Green coffee for filter roasting; or roasted whole bean for cafés |
| Profile | "Floral and citrus, light body" or "chocolate, low acidity for espresso blends" |
| Process | Washed, natural, or open to both |
| Quality level | An export grade, a minimum cup score under a named protocol, or "commercial, blend component" |
| Quantity | Approximate volume per order and per year, with units |
| Destination | Country and port, and whether you import directly |
| Timing | When you need the coffee shipped or delivered |
| Constraints | Certifications, packaging, labelling or documents you need |

Share how you evaluate coffee too: your roast style for samples, and whether you cup, brew, or both. It helps the supplier choose suitable options.

## Step 2: Know which sample you are looking at

Not all samples mean the same thing. A **type sample** shows a general quality level. An **offer sample** represents a specific lot available now. A **pre-shipment sample** comes from the export-ready coffee just before it ships. An **arrival sample** is taken after the coffee lands. Ask the supplier to label each sample with its type, lot reference and date, and keep that information with your notes.

## Step 3: Log samples as they arrive

Record, for every sample:

- date received, sample type and lot reference;
- the weight received and the condition of the packaging;
- for roasted samples, the roast date printed on the pack.

A simple spreadsheet is enough. Consistent records let you compare lots fairly and match a pre-shipment sample to the offer sample you approved.

## Step 4: Assess green samples physically

Before roasting, look at and smell the green coffee. Off odours, such as musty or fermented smells, are worth noting straight away.

If you have the equipment, measure **moisture content** and **water activity**. The International Coffee Organization recommends 8% to 12.5% moisture for exported green coffee. Values outside that range deserve a question to the supplier.

For a **defect count**, the SCA's long-standing green grading method sorts a 350 g sample and classifies defects into two categories. Under that method, specialty grade allowed zero Category 1 defects and no more than five full Category 2 defects. Even if you don't grade formally, noting broken, insect-damaged or discoloured beans gives useful feedback.

## Step 5: Roast and cup consistently

Roast all samples in a comparable way, typically a light, even sample roast, so that differences come from the coffee rather than the roast.

Cupping is the standard way to compare coffees. The long-established SCA protocol uses 8.25 g of coffee per 150 ml of water at about 93 °C, with the crust broken after about four minutes. Use several cups per sample to check consistency, and cup samples blind if you can.

In 2024 the SCA adopted its **Coffee Value Assessment (CVA)**, which separates *describing* a coffee (what attributes are present, and how intense they are) from *valuing* it (how much you like it). Whatever form you use, tell your supplier which protocol you followed, so your scores can be interpreted correctly.

## Step 6: Evaluate roasted samples as your customers will

For roasted coffee, brew it the way it will be used: espresso, filter, or both. Taste it on more than one day after the roast date to see how it develops, and check the packaging: seal, valve, labelling and batch coding. Note the time between the roast date and when you received the sample, because a commercial shipment will take at least as long.

## Step 7: Give feedback a supplier can act on

Specific, structured feedback is much more useful than a single score. For each sample, share:

- **Decision:** approve, reject, or "close, but…".
- **What you liked:** the attributes you want more of.
- **What didn't fit:** for example "too fermented for our customers" or "acidity higher than our espresso blend needs".
- **Physical issues:** moisture, defects or packaging problems, with numbers if you have them.
- **Next step:** request a similar lot, a pre-shipment sample, or a quotation.

When you approve a coffee, **confirm the approval in writing with the lot reference**, and keep some of the approved sample. If the pre-shipment or arrival sample differs, you then have a clear reference point.
